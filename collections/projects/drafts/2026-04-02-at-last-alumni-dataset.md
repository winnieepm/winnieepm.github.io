## project context

### why this matters

Worrying about the job market one day got me wondering about the places Scholars' Lab alumni go to work after their time at UVA. It's been a long while since I stopped dreaming of a tenure-track job, but twelve years after Katina Rogers published "Humanities Unbound: Supporting Careers and Scholarship Beyond the Tenure Track" the alt-ac path remains murky and getting murkier with the diversification of digital humanities positions, the massive funding cuts in higher ed, and the evermore-accelerated unchecked growth of AI's role in all our lives.

This being the case for academic-adjacent positions, what are the chances for humanities PhDs to learn about potentially suitable industry, freelance, or other job possibilities? A knee-jerk ick reaction to these has, and continues to be, taught and deeply ingrained into us, after all. Those are the jobs for the losers who don't make it into the inner sanctum of tenure, can't even land teaching positions, or aren't resilient enough to endure the adjunct path long enough. Yea, right. This is what I've heard and sensed over and over again in the past 10 years, sometimes directly stated and others implied in a number of creative ways, like the way many academic departments conspicuously forget to announce the job placements of alumni who take alt-ac or non-ac jobs in their internal listservs.

Amidst this atmosphere, the [Scholars' Lab](#) within the [Digital Humanities Center](#) at the University of Virginia brings a different vision: to meet students where they are, ask them where they want to be, and help them develop the professional skills to get there. They support this vision using digital humanities as their framework, a discipline characterized by digital literacy as much as it is by a desire to recognize the labor conditions of academic work.
